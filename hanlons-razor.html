Hanlon's Razor

“Never attribute to malice that which is adequately explained by stupidity.”

When something goes wrong and a person is involved, the boring explanation is usually the right one: they were busy, distracted, uninformed or wrong. Deliberate harm happens, but it's rarer than it feels in the moment. Below, a crowd of people who all did the same annoying thing gets sorted by why they did it.

    Where it comes from

    A joke submission that stuck

    Robert J. Hanlon sent the line to Arthur Bloch for Murphy's Law Book Two (1980), a collection of tongue-in-cheek laws about why things go wrong. The idea is older: Goethe wrote in 1774 that misunderstandings and neglect cause more trouble than wickedness, and Robert Heinlein made the same point in a 1941 story. It's called a "razor" because, like Occam's, it shaves away an explanation you don't need.

    Read "stupidity" generously. In practice it means ordinary error: not knowing, not noticing, forgetting, being overloaded, or having different priorities than you assumed.

    Why it usually holds

    The crowd is mostly harmless

    Base rates

    Mistakes are cheap and constant. Malice takes motive, effort and attention on you specifically. Across everyone who might have done it, the careless vastly outnumber the cruel.

    You see outcomes, not intent

    A missed email looks identical whether it was ignored or buried. Your brain fills the gap with a story, and stories about villains are more vivid than stories about inboxes.

    The costs are lopsided

    Wrongly accusing someone damages a relationship that may be hard to repair. Wrongly giving the benefit of the doubt is cheap and easy to revise once more evidence arrives.

    Where it stops cutting

    The key word is “adequately”

    The razor is a starting assumption, not a verdict. It tells you to begin with error and move off it only when error stops explaining what you see. Drag the repeat slider above and watch the red group grow: one incident says little, but a pattern is evidence.

    Repetition

    One bad merge is a mistake. The same person reverting your work six sprints in a row is a pattern that error explains poorly.

    Incentives

    If the "mistake" always lands in the other person's favour, ask who benefits. Errors are random; self-serving errors are suspicious.

    Adversarial settings

    Phishing emails, fraud and security incidents are full of people trying to look clumsy. There the base rate of malice is high, so the razor doesn't apply.

    The arithmetic behind the slider

    Updating on a pattern

    Start with the chance it was deliberate, the red share for the situation you picked (around 5%). Assume someone acting on purpose would do it again 90% of the time, and someone careless would repeat the same slip 30% of the time. Each repeat multiplies the odds toward malice by 3.

    The exact numbers matter less than the shape: a low prior survives one incident, starts to wobble at three, and gives way by four or five. That's the razor working as intended.

    Related ideas

    Its neighbours

    Occam's razor
    Prefer the explanation that needs the fewest assumptions. Malice needs a motive, a plan and a target; error needs only a person.
    Fundamental attribution error
    We blame other people's behaviour on their character and our own on circumstances. Hanlon's razor is a deliberate correction for it.
    Grey's law
    A reply in the spirit of Clarke: incompetence that is advanced enough looks the same as malice. Sometimes the effect on you is identical, and you should respond to the effect.
